# Exercise 1: Recognizing Canonical Curves

## Problem Statement
Identify four curves, their principal features, and the information needed to sketch them.

## Theoretical Background and Concepts
Equal positive squared terms give a circle; unequal positive normalized terms give an ellipse; one squared variable gives a parabola; opposite signs give a hyperbola.

### How to Think About This Problem

Try to reach canonical form before naming the object. Complete squares to reveal translations, read denominators as squared semiaxis lengths, and use the signs of squared terms to distinguish ellipses, hyperbolas, and parabolas. For a surface, hold one coordinate constant and recognize the resulting two-dimensional cross-section.

## Detailed Step-by-Step Solution
1. $x^2+y^2=9$: circle, center $(0,0)$, radius $3$; intercepts $(\pm3,0),(0,\pm3)$.
2. $x^2/16+y^2/9=1$: ellipse, center $0$, semiaxes $4$ horizontal and $3$ vertical.
3. $y^2=8x=4(2)x$: right-opening parabola, vertex $0$, axis $y=0$.
4. $x^2/9-y^2/4=1$: horizontal hyperbola, vertices $(\pm3,0)$, asymptotes $y=\pm(2/3)x$.

## Common Mistakes and Key Takeaway

A common mistake is reading a denominator as the semiaxis instead of its square root, or naming a surface from memory without checking whether real sections exist. Build sketches from dependable landmarks: center or vertex, intercepts, axes, asymptotes, and several cross-sections. Substituting these landmarks provides a direct consistency check.

## Verification and Consistency Checks
The listed intercepts satisfy their equations, and the signs and denominators predict the stated orientations and scales.
